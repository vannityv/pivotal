01

How the world has changed

  • The flip
  • What’s next for developers
  • Why the process has to move

Development used to be 90% doing and 10% thinking. That has flipped, and product development processes need to change to suit it.

The doing is now fast and cheap. Previously developers needed to know the language, the framework, the patterns. The thinking mattered, but it was the small part of the day.

That 10% of thinking is now the critical skill.

What’s next for developers

Developers start from a strong position. That logical mindset takes years to build and it's the hard part to replace. Breaking a problem down, spotting the edge case, telling a good solution from one that only looks good.

What changes is where it gets applied. Most developers will need to build real product and implementation skill, which means understanding the customer, taking a rough intent and turning it into something real, and knowing which questions to ask before building anything. The alternative is to go further the other way and get deeply technical: architecture, infrastructure, integrations, the data model, judging whether generated code is safe, and owning the guidance the AI works from.

Both are good careers. Most people will lean one way and keep enough of the other to be useful.

What shrinks is the middle, where the decision was already made and someone had to type it out.

Why the process has to move

Long specs, estimates, planning ceremonies and careful scoping all existed to protect engineering time, because engineering time was the expensive thing. Keeping them in place spends the care in the wrong spot.

PIVoTAL is what we use at COHO instead.

90% doing, 10% thinking. Now it's the other way round.

02

Who this is for

This is for people keen to embrace the change, as long as the change is safe and leads to better products. Faster on its own isn't the goal.

If you'd rather defend the old process, and not embrace the change that AI is bringing, PIVoTAL isn't for you.

It works at any size, because anything large breaks down into pieces small enough for one person to hold in their head. How do you eat an elephant? One bite at a time. There's a section below on running it on something big.

The process

Six stages. Each has one person accountable, a defined exit, and a defined way back. The o is silent, like MoSCoW.

It starts with Product, deliberately. The companies that build the best things are the ones where product leads and everything else serves it. Product is first in the name and first in the flow because that's where the thinking starts.

The line between Product and Implement runs both ways, all the time. The dashed lines are the less frequent routes back, and each one is a normal event rather than a failure.

Product

One person writes the spec. The problem, who has it, what the change does, what it explicitly doesn't do, and how we'll know it worked.

ProductProduct Lead is accountable

Implement

The spec becomes something working on screen, by prompting AI against our development and design guidelines, with unit tests written as you go rather than bolted on later. The Product Lead stays involved throughout, because this is where the questions the spec never answered come up. Hand-written code is the exception and needs a reason.

ImplementationImplementation Lead is accountable

Product review. Does it do what we wanted? Spec coverage, and whether the flow makes sense for what the customer is doing. Product + UX

Design review. Does it look right? Visual fit with the rest of the product, the design language, the styling rules. Design + UX

Run in parallelBoth must sign off before Test

Test

Somebody uses it properly and tries to break it. Zero, one, a thousand. Part-filled records. Notifications reaching the right people. Different permissions and account setups.

Assure

Code review. Is it safe, does it fit the codebase, and is the test coverage good enough? AI reviews first, a human reviews the review. It must not change function or appearance.

Implement is a conversation, not a handover

This is the part most teams get wrong, and it matters more than any other rule here.

A product spec describes what something should do. It rarely describes what it should be. As soon as someone starts building, questions appear that the spec never answered. What happens to the records that already exist. Where this sits in the navigation. What the empty state says. Whether this is two screens or one.

So Implement is not a stage where someone takes the spec away and comes back with a feature. The Product Lead stays in it. Build a bit, show it, ask, adjust, show it again. That loop can run several times in a day, and it should.

The flow is drawn top to bottom, which makes it look like a waterfall. It isn't. The line between Product and Implement is the busiest one on the diagram and it runs both ways, which is why it's the only solid arrow pointing back up.

Now and then the intent really is clear enough that nothing needs raising until review. That's rare. Assuming you're in that case is the most common way a change goes wrong.

Two roles carry it

The Product Lead is the person leading the idea. They wrote the spec, they answer questions all the way through Implement, and they own whether this was the right thing to build at all. The Implementation Lead runs the build and carries the change through to Live. Everyone else gives a verdict.

Nobody signs off their own work. That's the only hard rule.

Changes get smaller as work moves down

Product can change anything. Implement changes how it's built. Validate changes function and appearance. Test changes nothing and reports. Assure changes only code, with no visible footprint.

If a stage wants to make a change that belongs higher up, it sends it back. That's what stops the last three stages quietly redesigning the product.

Handing it back

A process that only moves forward is one people lie to. Naming each way back is what keeps everyone comfortable using them.

FromBack toWhen
ImplementProductConstantly. Any question the spec did not answer
ValidateImplementFunction or appearance needs fixing
ValidateProductThe spec itself was wrong
TestImplementA defect is found
AssureTestA code change carries any behavioural risk
AssureAI guidanceThe AI got it wrong because our guidance was unclear

Re-entry is targeted. A fix that changes nothing visible goes back to Test only. A fix that changes function or appearance goes back through the review first.

The usual failure isn't a bad review. It's silence, with work sitting on someone who hasn't looked at it. The Implementation Lead chases. If they go quiet, the Product Lead picks it up. That's the whole escalation path.

Running it on something big

A six month project doesn't go through PIVoTAL as one change. It gets specced once at a high level, then broken into areas small enough that one person can hold each in their head.

The high level spec answers the questions that only make sense at the top. What are we building, for whom, what does it replace, what are the hard edges. It doesn't try to describe every screen, because it can't, and pretending otherwise is how the old way went wrong.

Each area then runs the full loop on its own. Product, Implement, Validate, Test, Assure, Live. Areas can run at the same time if they don't overlap, and the Product Lead stays the same person across all of them so the thing holds together.

How do you eat an elephant? One bite at a time. The point of breaking it down isn't project management, it's that a small piece can actually be validated by a human looking at a screen.

Why Assure gets easier every month

Code review is the stage people expect to grow into a tax, because output grows and someone has to read all of it. It only works that way if you start from "AI code is bad".

So when the reviewer wants something changed, they ask a second question. Why did the AI write it this way? It's nearly always one of five things, and only the last is a dead end.

  • Our guidance didn't cover this case
  • Our guidance covered it ambiguously
  • The existing pattern wasn't discoverable from the prompt or the codebase
  • The prompt left out something the guidance assumes
  • The guidance is right and this was a one-off

Everything but the last one changes the guidance, the docs, or how we prompt. Review stops being a gate and becomes how the team gets better at instructing the thing that writes its code.

The measure is the share of changes passing Assure with no changes required. It should climb month on month. Flat means it isn't working.

Track the reasons as well as the rate. Lots of "didn't cover this" means you're in new territory. Lots of "ambiguous" means your docs need rewriting, not extending. One named person owns the guidance, so findings land there instead of being fixed once and forgotten.

04

Skills

  • The five skills
  • Rating out of 10
  • The scorecard
  • Staffing a change

A title says what someone was hired to do. A skill says what they can be handed. Only the second one helps you decide who does what.

Product

Knowing what should be built and why. Customer needs, and what the business is trying to achieve. Higher up it means commercial judgement: not just whether a feature is good, but whether it's worth a month right now.

Implementation

Prompting AI to build the product. Specs always miss detail, so this is the extra set of eyes. Knowing the existing building blocks and putting the right one in the right place instead of inventing a new pattern every time.

Design

Whether it looks right and fits the product. Early on it also means checking the design files and making sure the styling follows the rules.

UX

How it feels. Using it and asking whether it makes sense, whether it's easy, whether it asks for the right thing at the right moment. UX sits across both reviews rather than being a stage of its own.

Technical

Dev ops, reading code and judging it, understanding the limits of the tech you and your partners depend on. Sometimes needed in Implement, always needed in Assure, and needed to keep the AI guidance clear.

Rating out of 10

A number on its own just starts an argument. What makes it usable is everyone agreeing what each band means. Most people are strong in two or three and have a zero somewhere. That's normal.

ScoreBandWhat it means in any skill
0–1NoneNo working capability here
2–3AwareFollows someone else's work. Can't carry it
4–5Capable with supportFine on straightforward work. Needs checking, and help when it's unfamiliar
6–7IndependentCarries normal work start to finish. Knows when they're out of their depth
8–9StrongHandles unfamiliar and complex work. Others come to them
10Sets the standardDefines how the team does this and improves it

Self rate first, then calibrate. A score needs a recent example behind it, not a feeling.

Publish them. Secret scores make staffing look arbitrary, and people assume the worst about themselves.

Keep it away from performance review. This measures what someone can be handed, not how well they're doing. A four in four skills and a nine in the fifth can be your most valuable person.

Keep tens rare. If half the team is a ten at something, the scale has stopped being useful.

The scorecard

A printable sheet for rating one person across the five skills. It keeps the last score alongside the new one, small and quiet, so you can see which way someone is moving.

05

Adopting it

  • Five steps
  • What counts as a change
  • Write the guidance files first

The risk is that it arrives all at once, everyone follows it for two weeks, and then it quietly stops.

  1. Agree the stages, the skill definitions and the thresholds. Changes now are cheap.
  2. Rate the team. Self rate, calibrate, publish.
  3. Run it on the next two or three changes. Pick a big one, because easy ones won't find the gaps.
  4. Review what broke. It'll be handoffs and thresholds. Adjust.
  5. Make it the default and start tracking the pass rate.

What counts as a change

Decide early what counts as a meaningful change. If everything goes through the full process it becomes a bottleneck and people will route around it. Ours is anything a customer would notice, and anything touching data, money or permissions. A typo fix doesn't, but it still gets Assure.

Incidents and urgent customer fixes keep whatever route they have now. Making one process serve both is how processes get abandoned.

Write the guidance files first

PIVoTAL is AI-first, so the quality of what comes out is the quality of what you put in. Before running this on anything real, write down how your codebase works.

Most tools now read an AGENTS.md at the root of the repo, and some read their own equivalent. The filename matters less than the habit. It's where you tell the AI what it would otherwise have to guess.

  • The stack, and the conventions you actually follow rather than the ones you meant to
  • Where things live, and which existing patterns should be reused instead of reinvented
  • How you name things
  • What your tests look like and what has to be covered
  • What's off limits, and what needs a human before it's touched

It's never finished, and that's the point. Every finding from Assure goes back into it. Over time it becomes the most valuable file in the repo, because it's the one deciding what the next hundred changes look like.
